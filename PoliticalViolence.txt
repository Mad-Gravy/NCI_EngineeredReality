On Sept. 10, Charlie Kirk, a right-wing American activist and ally of President Donald J. Trump, was fatally shot at Utah Valley University.

Kirk was touring multiple college campuses across the nation and starting debates on campus with Turning Point USA (TPUSA), his nonprofit organization. Kirk built his media empire in 2012 after dropping out of Harper College in Illinois. Shortly after, TPUSA was born. Kirk’s brand expanded as he moved beyond campus debates to podcasts and YouTube channels, including the popular debate channel, Jubilee. Kirk was famously known for having conservative and sometimes extreme ideals regarding multiple topics including abortion, gun control, diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) programs and LGBTQ rights, to which many have agreed and disagreed with.

The individual who is suspected to have shot Kirk seemingly strongly opposed the ideology Kirk promoted and decided to answer that ideology with violence. While I found the nature of the situation to be ironic regarding Kirk’s own ideology on gun violence, I found the public’s response to be fairly interesting.

There seemed to be a large spectrum of feelings regarding Kirk’s passing. Some mourned, others celebrated and some felt indifferent to the situation at large. However, there were two prevailing messages across the discourse: Political violence in America is on the rise, and no one should die because of their political opinion.

While I agree with these sentiments, they bring up many questions. What is political violence? Whose violence actually matters? Why are some attacks labeled crimes while others are dismissed as mere disagreements?

While these questions seem like they can be easily answered, the definitions of “violence,” “opinion” and “freedom” are all interpreted in different ways between the left and right, especially when observing the ideological view that a person holds. In observing the reactions to the assassination of Kirk, it is imperative to understand what political violence is.

According to the United Nations (UN), political violence is defined as “hostile, aggressive or violent acts motivated by political objectives or a desire to directly or indirectly affect political change or change in governance.” Political violence isn’t limited to a singular party or ideology — it encompasses any aggression motivated by political aims from a group, individual or state. However, this perception that political violence has emerged only recently is just a myth because political violence is ingrained in our history.

Pistol dueling was a common practice among people and politicians to settle disputes from the 17th through the 19th centuries. Alexander Hamilton, a founding father, was notably killed by Aaron Burr in 1804 from a pistol duel because of their political and personal rivalry. However, not all of these acts were necessarily fatal. In 1856, Rep. Preston Brooks beat Sen. Charles Sumner with a cane until he was unconscious on the floor of the United States Senate Chamber over a dispute on slavery.

Political violence isn’t a new era emerging — it is a byproduct of ideological divisions and natural social unrest.

This is not to say that there hasn’t been a drastic increase in political violence. Within the first six months of 2025 alone, there were approximately 150 politically motivated attacks, which is almost double the amount during the same time last year. These drastic numbers of violent political action are a result of the tense, polarized political climate in the U.S., and the media only exacerbates it. The rise of social media and outrage-driven news has amplified the effects of political aggression.

Professor Jonathan Anzalone, assistant director at the Center for News Literacy in the School of Communication and Journalism (SoCJ) at Stony Brook University, said, “In terms of paths to radicalization and extreme steps, there are far more avenues to pursue and find that kind of radical talk, extreme speech and encouragement for extreme steps than people had 60 or 100 years ago.”  

Now more than ever, we are seeing social media becoming the host by harboring these radical and extreme ideologies. Platforms like X, TikTok, Facebook and Instagram have caused the lines of extremism to blur rapidly. Algorithms push and reward outrage and create emotional tensions. Users are constantly pushed into political echo chambers where anyone with opposing political views experiences dehumanization.

Anzalone described the current environment as being filled with “a lot of incentives to be extreme,” pointing out that social media algorithms, heated opinion content and talk radio ensure that incentives are in place “to be as extreme as possible because [when] you get attention, you might get money [or] you might get a show.”

Violence is normalized as a defense, because the views in these echo chambers begin to drive people’s identity. This “defense” and online rhetoric quickly and aggressively escalates as arguments lead to threats, and threats lead to action. Political disagreement is a spectacle. Conflict becomes a performance for attention, fostering a negatively polarized environment of extremism in both the left and right. As a result, violence has not only become easier to organize, but also easier to justify.

This heightened visibility of violence has changed the conversations surrounding it. The stream of viral outrage regarding Kirk’s assassination and the constant political violence messaging poses a deeper question: Why are certain acts only now being labeled as “political violence?”

America is no stranger to using politically motivated aggression, especially against marginalized communities. During the Reconstruction era, white supremacist groups like the Ku Klux Klan (KKK) performed heinous acts against freed Black Americans. These acts weren’t just vocal outrage — lynchings, arson and beatings were often used to intimidate Black citizens from voting or asserting their basic civil rights. Local authorities turned a blind eye and allowed the violence to continue with impunity.

Almost a century later, state-sanctioned violence persists. In 1965, peaceful marchers in Selma, Ala. were brutally beaten by police officers during what is now known as “Bloody Sunday” in an attempt to suppress the fight for voting rights. Within the same era, the FBI’s COINTELPRO program was designed to surveil and harass civil rights organizations and activists. Civil rights leaders like Martin Luther King Jr. and Fred Hampton of the Black Panther Party were categorized as major threats to national security.

Violence in Indigenous communities has the same pattern. In 1973, members of the American Indian Movement (AIM) occupied and forcibly took over the Wounded Knee reservation. They demanded the U.S. government review all treaties made with Indigenous people and investigate the treatment of Native Americans. The federal government responded by deploying federal marshals and conducting sieges that killed two protestors. A few decades later, during the Dakota Access pipeline protests at Standing Rock in 2016, activists and allies faced militarized police and rubber bullets during a peaceful protest of the environmental injustices to their sacred tribal land.

These are only a few of the oppressive and extreme forces used to suppress dissent among marginalized populations. In all these case studies, violence against the people was politically motivated and mostly stemmed from the government itself. Violence was used to silence dissent and maintain white supremacist hierarchical power structures to suppress minorities.

In contrast, the killing of Kirk sparked national outrage demanding calls for unity and a culmination of press coverage portraying the incident as a threat to democracy itself. It was actually baffling to see the sheer amount of governmental response over the following days.

Firstly, President Trump announced that Kirk would receive the Presidential Medal of Freedom, which is considered the U.S. government’s highest civilian honor. It is typically presented to individuals “to recognize a lifetime of significant achievements in the arts, public service, science or other fields.” Shortly after, Trump also ordered U.S. flags to be flown at half-staff on all public buildings, military posts and naval vessels in respect to his death.

The legislative branch has also moved to commemorate Kirk, as the Senate passed a resolution to designate Oct. 14, Kirk’s birthday, as the “National Day of Remembrance for Charlie Kirk.” Similarly, the House of Representatives passed a resolution honoring his legacy. Furthermore, the “Charlie Kirk Commemorative Coin Act” was recently introduced in the House to authorize the mining of 400,000 silver dollar coins in 2026 to honor Kirk.

Quite frankly, I find this response ridiculous. Do I agree his death was tragic? Yes. But the lengths that are being taken to commemorate Kirk are simply shocking, especially considering the fact that he was not a government figure.

Where was this response when Sen. John Hoffman of Minnesota and his wife, Yvette, were shot and injured in their home or when State House Speaker Melissa Hortman and her husband, Mark, were killed in their home in Minnesota? These instances barely received actual governmental recognition.

When Trump was asked if he would lower the flags in honor of Hortman, he responded suggesting he was “not familiar” with the case and saying, “Well, if the governor [Minnesota Governor Tim Walz] had asked me to do that, I would have done that.”

Why is it that this one individual on the far right gets more attention than politicians on the opposite side of the aisle? It’s not about the act itself, but rather who it happens to. Violence against those in power, whether in government or in ideological beliefs, becomes a crisis for democracy. However, violence against marginalized groups is just background noise.

The nation is not just suddenly growing more violent — its violence is only more visible when it fits the correct political narrative. As a society, we have now defined whose pain is “necessary” and whose pain is “unacceptable.” The progression of social media has only amplified the impacts of these patterns.

Algorithms have now adopted the same ideological machinery, telling us who is worthy of empathy and who is not. When protestors in 2020 advocated for police reform following the murder of George Floyd and Breonna Taylor, many prominent media outlets framed the protests as chaos. But when prominent conservative figures are killed, it becomes an “assault on democracy.” So which is it? This distinction isn’t about morality or legality — it is about ideology.

We are not defining violence by harm done. We are defining it by the identity and power of its victims. This double standard is a normalizing force against our political peers as governance. The disdain and wedge driven between parties fosters a false narrative of “true American patriotism,” transforming violence from a universal wrong to a weapon of partisanship.

As Americans, if we truly want to confront political violence, we must first acknowledge that this notion is not new — it is a part of the nation’s bloodstream. To heal as a nation, we must be honest with ourselves about the history of our nation.

America has always been selective about whose suffering counts, and outrage for victims is never evenly distributed. Violence has always been justified if it fits a particular narrative the country wants to uphold. Until we can face the truth, we are not confronting violence — we are competing for control over its narrative.
